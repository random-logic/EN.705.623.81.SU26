# 1. Case Summary

In 2023, two New York lawyers and their law firm were sanctioned after submitting a legal brief that included fake case citations generated by ChatGPT. The brief was part of a personal injury lawsuit against the airline Avianca. ChatGPT produced several legal cases that appeared realistic but did not actually exist. The lawyers submitted those citations to the court and continued to rely on them even after opposing counsel and the judge questioned whether the cases were real.

The people affected included the client, the opposing party, the court, and the legal profession more broadly. The client’s case was dismissed separately because it was filed too late, but the use of false citations damaged trust in the legal process. AI played the role of an unreliable research assistant: it generated convincing but false legal information, and the lawyers failed to properly verify it before using it in an official court filing.



# 2. Responsible AI Analysis

Several Responsible AI principles were involved in this case.

## Accuracy and Reliability

The most obvious issue was accuracy. ChatGPT generated fake legal cases, fake quotations, and incorrect citations. In legal work, accuracy is extremely important because court filings can affect people’s rights, financial outcomes, and access to justice. A tool that produces plausible but false information creates serious risk when users treat its output as factual.

## Accountability

Accountability was also central to the case. The judge stated that there is nothing inherently improper about lawyers using AI for assistance, but attorneys still have a gate-keeping responsibility to verify their filings. Even though ChatGPT generated the fake cases, the lawyers were accountable for submitting them to the court. Responsible AI does not remove human responsibility, especially in high-stakes professional settings.

## Transparency

Transparency was involved because the court and opposing counsel were not initially made aware that AI had been used to generate the legal research. If the lawyers had disclosed their use of ChatGPT earlier, the court may have better understood how the fake citations appeared in the brief. Transparency about AI use is important when AI-generated material influences official decisions or legal arguments.

## Human Oversight

This case shows a failure of meaningful human oversight. The lawyers relied on ChatGPT’s answers without independently confirming the cases through reliable legal databases such as Westlaw, LexisNexis, Bloomberg Law, or official court records. Human review existed in theory, because lawyers submitted the brief, but it was not effective review.

## Safety and Harm Prevention

Although this was not a physical safety issue, it was still a safety issue in a broader Responsible AI sense. AI-generated misinformation in the legal system can harm clients, waste court resources, mislead judges, and undermine public trust. In high-stakes domains such as law, medicine, and finance, hallucinated information can cause serious real-world harm.

## Governance

The case also raises governance concerns. The law firm apparently lacked clear internal policies for using generative AI in legal work. There were no strong approval gates, verification requirements, or AI-use guidelines that prevented unverified AI output from entering a court filing. Good governance would have required documented procedures for AI-assisted legal research.



# 3. Root Cause Investigation

Several failures occurred across the AI development and deployment lifecycle, as well as in the law firm’s use of the tool.

## Model Limitation: Hallucination

ChatGPT is designed to generate likely text based on patterns in data, not to guarantee factual truth. When asked for legal cases, it can produce citations that look authentic but are fabricated. This is known as hallucination. The model did not have a built-in guarantee that every citation corresponded to a real case.

## Inappropriate Use in a High-Stakes Context

The lawyers used a general-purpose chatbot for legal research, a high-stakes professional task requiring verified sources. ChatGPT was not designed to replace legal databases or professional legal judgment. The deployment context mattered: a tool that might be acceptable for brainstorming or drafting became risky when used as a source of legal authority.

## Lack of Verification

The biggest human-process failure was the lack of verification. The lawyers should have checked every case citation using trusted legal research tools or official court databases. When they could not find the cases, that should have been treated as a warning sign. Instead, they relied on ChatGPT’s responses and continued defending the citations after questions were raised.

## Over-trust in AI

The lawyers appeared to assume that ChatGPT functioned like a search engine or legal database. This shows a misunderstanding of how generative AI works. The model can produce confident-sounding answers even when it is wrong. The users over-trusted the tool’s output because it sounded authoritative.

## Poor Organizational AI Governance

The law firm likely did not have a clear policy for AI use. There seemed to be no requirement that attorneys disclose AI assistance internally, verify AI-generated citations, or receive training before using AI in legal work. Without firm-level governance, individual attorneys were left to make risky decisions on their own.

## Insufficient Documentation and Warnings

Another contributing factor was the lack of clear user understanding about the tool’s limitations. While ChatGPT may include general warnings, users in professional settings need stronger reminders that it can fabricate sources. For legal research specifically, the system should make clear that citations must be independently verified.

## Failure to Respond Properly After Detection

The issue became worse after opposing counsel and the court questioned the citations. At that point, the lawyers had an opportunity to investigate, correct the record, and withdraw the false cases. Instead, according to the judge, they continued to stand by the fake opinions. This turned an initial technology misuse into a more serious professional responsibility failure.



# 4. Solution Design

This issue could have been prevented through a combination of technical safeguards and governance policies.

## Technical Fixes

### Citation Verification Tools

A technical fix would be to require legal citations generated by AI to be automatically checked against authoritative legal databases. If the AI provides a case name, docket number, court, or citation, the system should verify that the case exists before presenting it as legal authority. If verification fails, the tool should clearly label the result as unverified or refuse to provide it as a real citation.

### Retrieval-Augmented Generation

A safer design would use retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, connected to trusted legal databases. Instead of generating legal citations from memory, the AI would retrieve actual cases from verified sources and then summarize them. The system should provide links to the original documents so lawyers can inspect the source directly.

### Uncertainty Warnings and Refusal Behavior

The AI system should include stronger uncertainty thresholds for legal research. If it cannot verify a case, it should say something like: “I cannot confirm that this case exists. Do not cite it without checking an official legal database.” In high-risk domains, the model should avoid inventing citations and should refuse to provide unsupported legal authorities.

### Red-Teaming for Legal Hallucinations

Before being used in legal contexts, AI systems should be tested against known failure modes, including fake citations, fake quotes, incorrect case names, and misidentified judges. Red-teaming could help identify prompts that cause the model to hallucinate legal authorities and improve safeguards.

### Audit Logs

Legal AI tools should keep audit logs showing what prompts were entered, what answers were generated, and whether citations were verified. This would support accountability and make it easier to investigate incidents if inaccurate information enters a legal document.



## Governance and Policy Fixes

### Mandatory Human Verification

Law firms should adopt a policy requiring attorneys to verify every AI-generated legal citation through official or trusted legal research platforms before including it in a filing. AI output should be treated as a draft or lead, not as an authority.

### AI Use Approval Gates

Before any AI-assisted legal brief is filed with a court, there should be an approval gate. A supervising attorney or designated reviewer should confirm that all citations, quotations, and legal claims have been checked. This is especially important when junior lawyers or staff use generative AI tools.

### Training on Generative AI Limits

Lawyers and legal staff should receive training on how generative AI works, including hallucinations, false confidence, privacy risks, and professional responsibility duties. Users need to understand that ChatGPT is not the same as Westlaw, LexisNexis, or a court database.

### Clear Internal AI Policy

The law firm should create a written AI policy explaining which tools may be used, for what tasks, and under what conditions. For example, AI could be allowed for brainstorming, outlining, or simplifying language, but not for final legal research unless connected to verified legal sources.

### Disclosure and Documentation

Firms should document when AI is used in preparing legal filings. Depending on court rules, they may also need to disclose AI use to the court. Even when disclosure is not required, internal documentation helps maintain accountability.

### Incident Response Plan

If an AI-generated error is discovered, the firm should have a clear incident response plan. This should include immediately investigating the issue, notifying the court if necessary, correcting the filing, informing the client, and reviewing how the error happened to prevent repetition.



# Conclusion

The ChatGPT fake legal citations case demonstrates that generative AI can create serious risks when used without verification in high-stakes professional settings. The main problem was not simply that ChatGPT hallucinated, but that humans relied on its output without proper oversight. Responsible AI requires both safer technical systems and strong professional governance. In legal work, AI can be useful as an assistant, but final responsibility must remain with trained professionals who verify every factual and legal claim before submitting it.
